# High TSH (Possible Underactive Thyroid)
> AI-drafted, pending medical review. Educational only — not a medical diagnosis.

**Marker:** TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone) is made by the pituitary gland to tell the thyroid how much thyroid hormone to produce. It works like a thermostat: when the body senses too little thyroid hormone, it raises TSH to push the thyroid to work harder. Because of this feedback loop, a high TSH often points, somewhat counterintuitively, to an *under*-active thyroid.

**What an abnormal result may relate to:** A higher-than-normal TSH generally suggests the thyroid isn't producing enough hormone on its own, so the body is signaling it to do more — a pattern associated with hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid). The degree matters: a mildly high TSH with normal thyroid hormone levels (sometimes called "subclinical") is interpreted differently from a clearly high TSH with low thyroid hormones. TSH can also fluctuate temporarily, so a single high value is often confirmed with a repeat test and additional thyroid measurements.

**Common contributing factors:**
- **Autoimmune thyroid conditions** — the most common cause of an underactive thyroid in many populations.
- **Iodine imbalance** — both too little and, in some cases, too much iodine can affect thyroid function.
- **Certain medications.**
- **Recovery phase after some illnesses**, which can transiently shift TSH.
- **Post-pregnancy thyroid changes** in some women.

**Commonly associated symptoms:**
- Fatigue and sluggishness
- Weight gain or difficulty losing weight
- Feeling cold more easily than others
- Dry skin and hair thinning
- Constipation
- Low mood or mental "fog"

Mild cases often cause few or no clear symptoms.

**General (non-prescriptive) lifestyle context:**
- Thyroid function is largely a medical rather than a lifestyle matter, so there is no general diet that "fixes" a high TSH.
- Adequate but not excessive iodine (from a normal balanced diet) is sometimes mentioned, but supplementation should not be self-directed, as too much can be as problematic as too little.

**How concerned to be:** A mildly high TSH is common and often monitored with repeat testing rather than treated immediately, especially when thyroid hormone levels are still normal and symptoms are minimal. It carries more weight when TSH is clearly elevated, when thyroid hormones are low, or when symptoms are notable — situations generally considered reasons for medical follow-up. As a general pattern, thyroid results are confirmed and interpreted together (TSH plus T3/T4) rather than acted on from one number.

**Important:** This information is educational only and not a medical diagnosis. Please consult a licensed doctor to interpret your report and decide on any next steps.
